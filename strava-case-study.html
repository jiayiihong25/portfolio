Strava: The Pre-Activity Social Tool

Gamifying the pre-activity experience for intermittent Strava users to overcome the mental activation barrier and avoid skipping planned workouts.
Role: Product Designer
Date: March 2026
View Figma Presentation ↗

The Opportunity

The energy required to transition from a resting state to a workout-ready state represents a significant friction point for Strava’s largest user segment: the intermittent user. Unlike running fanatics who are intrinsically motivated by established rigid habit loops, these intermittent users possess the intent to exercise (evidenced by downloading Strava), but frequently fail at the point of execution due to immediate distractions or a lack of mood.

Currently, Strava caters primarily to the post-activity flex, leaving the intermittent user alone during the most difficult part of the journey: the critical 15-minute preparation window where they are most likely to skip in their mind and abandon their intent entirely.

Why is this a problem?

  • Barrier to Retention: When the app only supports the finish line, users start to associate Strava with the pressure of being a perfect athlete. For intermittent users who struggle with motivation, the app can become a negative reminder of failed plans, causing them to eventually disengage or delete the app to avoid guilt.
  • Mission Alignment Gap: Strava aims to connect athletes to what motivates them, but it currently offers no support during the hardest 15 minutes of the workout. Losing users to distractions right before a workout begins goes against the app's core purpose of helping people be active.
  • Losing the Battle for Attention: In the 15 minutes before a workout, Strava is in direct competition with social media and sedentary digital distractions. By remaining silent during this window, Strava allows users to choose low-effort entertainment over their physical health.

Audience & Goals

Primary Audience: Intermittent Strava users aged 18–34 who have genuine intent to exercise but frequently fail at execution due to low-activation moments, digital distractions, and an absence of social accountability.

Goals:

  • Maximize the feeling of social accountability and peer visibility in the 15-minute preparation window before a workout begins.
  • Minimize the temptation to choose a sedentary digital distraction (social media, couch) over starting the activity.
  • Minimize the back-and-forth communication required to coordinate workout timing with friends, removing the friction of group scheduling.

The Solution

A connected 4-part social accountability loop embedded in the Record tab that meets intermittent users before the workout begins and uses real-time peer visibility and social pressure to bridge the gap between intent and action.

  • 1. Lacing Up Notification
    When a friend toggles "Lacing Up," their connections receive a high-priority push notification inviting them off whatever they're currently doing and into Strava. This external trigger sources motivation from the social graph rather than the user's own willpower.
  • 2. Waiting on You Ping
    If the recipient does not confirm within 5 minutes, a second named push notification is automatically sent as part of the accountability loop (the "people are waiting" factor).
  • 3. Lacing Up Toggle
    Tapping either notification opens a full-screen prompt inside the app with a large "I'm Getting Ready!" button. A Group Status bar at the bottom shows friends' current states (Ready or Lacing Up) so the user can see exactly who is preparing, activating the psychological cost of backing out.
  • 4. Live Ready Room Module
    A persistent real-time module on the Record tab showing friends' current states with elapsed time. It provides the final push from preparation to pressing START by making peer activity visible and ambient.

User Interview Insights

  • Planning and scheduling: Most participants took a day-by-day approach to fitting in exercise, with workouts being the first thing cut during busy weeks. Group workouts were desired but rarely materialised due to no follow-through.
  • Motivation and the mental hurdle: Skipping was decided well before the workout window, driven by low mood and passive phone use. Knowing a specific friend was already getting ready, or was waiting on them, was the one consistent override.
  • Physical preparation and follow-through: Once participants began physically preparing, they almost always followed through. The decision to skip forms before any physical action is taken, making that pre-preparation window the critical intervention point.

Summary: The barrier is motivational, not logistical. What pushed users to act was a named peer already in motion or actively waiting on them.
